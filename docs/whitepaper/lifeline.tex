\begin{abstract}
Disasters remove exactly the infrastructure that messaging depends on: towers,
backhaul, power, and the reachable servers that hold our identities and
authorize our traffic. \emph{Project Lifeline} is a decentralized, offline-first,
end-to-end-encrypted messenger built as a Delay/Disruption-Tolerant Network
(DTN)~\cite{rfc4838} that keeps working while partitioned. It composes a
self-sovereign identity layer, a content-addressed bundle with a Merkle
manifest, a store-carry-forward router using binary spray-and-wait with custody
transfer, forward-secret sealed-sender encryption, a Kademlia overlay for online
discovery, and pluggable bridges to existing ecosystems (Nostr, Meshtastic).
This paper records the system's architecture and its four principal design
contributions: (i)~a \emph{residual differential transfer} framework that unifies
several bandwidth optimizations as $\Delta = Q - \mathbb{E}[Q\,|\,R]$;
(ii)~\emph{manifest-scoped HAVE swarm fetch}, a BitTorrent-over-DTN gap-fill that
completes an object from many providers over many carriers with no duplicate
traffic; (iii)~\emph{source-attributed custody reputation} for passively
detecting black-hole relays without weakening the delivery guarantee; and
(iv)~\emph{attenuable egress capabilities} with \emph{service-class} tiers, a
macaroon/Biscuit-style authorization token that governs internet access through
a gateway and---crucially for a mesh---is verified fully offline. We give a
security analysis mapped to a standard threat taxonomy, describe our evaluation
methodology and the benchmarks the DTN literature expects, and enumerate the
open problems (independent cryptographic review, comparative evaluation on
standard mobility traces, and quota/revocation for egress) that gate a
production deployment.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% ===========================================================================

The design center of most messaging systems is a reachable server. In a
disaster---an earthquake, a flood, a grid failure, a deliberate shutdown---that
assumption is precisely what fails. Project Lifeline targets the regime where
connectivity is \emph{intermittent, partitioned, and heterogeneous}: some nodes
share a short-range radio for minutes, some carry data physically between
partitions, and a lucky few briefly reach the wider internet.

We take the position, following the end-to-end argument~\cite{saltzer1984endtoend}
and the DTN architecture~\cite{rfc4838,rfc5050,rfc9171}, that reliability,
security, and authorization must live at the \emph{endpoints and in the data
they carry}, never in the links or in a server one must reach. Every design
decision in Lifeline is a consequence of that stance.

\paragraph{Contributions.} This paper documents:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=1pt]
  \item A layered architecture (\S\ref{sec:arch}) whose seams are narrow traits,
  so a new radio, overlay, or external network is a single implementation.
  \item Four named contributions: the \abstr{Residual Differential Transfer}
  framework (\S\ref{sec:diff}), \abstr{Manifest-Scoped HAVE Swarm Fetch}
  (\S\ref{sec:swarm}), \abstr{Source-Attributed Custody Reputation}
  (\S\ref{sec:custody}), and \abstr{Attenuable Egress Capabilities} with
  \abstr{Service-Class} tiers (\S\ref{sec:egress}).
  \item A security analysis mapped to a standard threat taxonomy
  (\S\ref{sec:security}) and an evaluation methodology grounded in the DTN
  benchmarking literature (\S\ref{sec:eval}).
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Status and honesty.} Lifeline is an implemented research system (a
Rust workspace of 15 crates, $\sim$200 automated tests, and an acceptance
simulation held to a $\geq 95\%$ delivery target). It has \emph{not} undergone
independent cryptographic review; where a guarantee is aspirational or a
mechanism is not yet wired into the live node, we say so explicitly.

\section{System and Threat Model}\label{sec:model}
% ===========================================================================

\paragraph{Network model.} The network is a time-varying graph whose edges (a
BLE contact, a Wi-Fi Aware link, a LoRa hop, an ultrasonic burst, an internet
tunnel, a bridged relay) appear and vanish. There is no assumption of a
contemporaneous end-to-end path; delivery is by \emph{store-carry-forward}. Each
edge type is an \abstr{Interface} with a maximum transmission unit and a
throughput class.

\paragraph{Adversary.} We assume a Dolev--Yao network adversary that can
observe, drop, replay, reorder, and inject on any link, plus a fraction of
malicious \emph{participants} who follow the wire format but misbehave (drop
bundles they accept, forge control messages, or attempt to spam). We assume the
endpoints' devices and long-term secret keys are not compromised; the standard
caveats of seizure and coercion apply and are discussed in \S\ref{sec:security}.
We explicitly do \emph{not} claim resistance to global traffic
analysis~\cite{vandenhooff2015vuvuzela,piotrowska2017loopix}.

\paragraph{Goals.} In priority order: (1)~\emph{life-safety delivery}---an SOS
must move even under spam pressure; (2)~\emph{confidentiality and integrity} of
message content and, as far as possible, of metadata; (3)~\emph{availability}
under partition; (4)~\emph{augmentation}, not replacement, of existing networks.

\section{Architecture}\label{sec:arch}
% ===========================================================================

Lifeline is organized as five layers, each a thin trait boundary
(Table~\ref{tab:layers}). The invariant that makes the system tractable is that
\emph{relays are dumb}: an intermediate node forwards an opaque, sealed
\abstr{Bundle} and can neither read it nor is trusted to have processed it
correctly. All correctness is re-established at the recipient.

\section{Identity and the Bundle}\label{sec:bundle}
% ===========================================================================

\subsection{Self-sovereign identity}
A node generates an Ed25519 signing key and an X25519 key-agreement key on first
launch. Its network \abstr{Address} is $\mathrm{BLAKE3}(\text{sign\_pub})[..16]$
---a 128-bit fingerprint of the public key. There is no registrar, phone number,
or email: the key \emph{is} the identity, which is what lets identity survive
when servers do not. Addresses double as the routing destination and the
Kademlia key, so the same 128-bit space underlies naming and discovery.

\subsection{Content addressing and the manifest}\label{sec:content}
Large objects are split into fixed-size blocks, each named by its BLAKE3 hash
---its \emph{content id} (CID)~\cite{benet2014ipfs}. An ordered \abstr{Manifest}
(itself named by a root CID over the tuple of length, block size, and block
CIDs) is a Merkle description of the whole object~\cite{merkle1987digsig}. This
buys three properties for free:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=1pt]
  \item \textbf{Intrinsic integrity} --- a received block is trusted only if it
  hashes to the requested CID; substitution is detected, not assumed away.
  \item \textbf{Global deduplication} --- identical content yields identical
  CIDs, so a block is carried once regardless of how many messages reference it
  or how many carriers deliver it.
  \item \textbf{A definition of ``done''} --- the object is complete iff the set
  of manifest CIDs the store is missing is empty; reassembly then verifies the
  whole object end-to-end.
\end{itemize}
For cross-partition resilience a bundle may additionally be protected with
Reed--Solomon erasure coding~\cite{reed1960polynomial}, so any $k$ of $n$ shards
reconstruct it even if some never arrive.

% ===========================================================================
\section{Routing}\label{sec:routing}
% ===========================================================================

\subsection{Store-carry-forward with binary spray-and-wait}
Lifeline routes with binary spray-and-wait~\cite{spyropoulos2005sprayandwait}, a
controlled-replication scheme that bounds the number of copies of a bundle to a
budget $L$: a node holding $c>1$ copies hands half to a new contact and keeps
half; a node holding one copy waits to deliver directly. This gives most of the
delivery probability of epidemic routing~\cite{vahdat2000epidemic} at a small
fraction of the transmission overhead---the metric that matters on
battery- and spectrum-limited devices.

\subsection{Priority and postage}
Bundles carry a \abstr{Priority} $\in\{$SOS, Alert, Normal, Bulk$\}$ that orders
scheduling. To resist flooding without ever delaying life-safety traffic, Normal
and Bulk bundles must carry Hashcash-style proof-of-work
``postage''~\cite{back2002hashcash} whose difficulty scales inversely with
priority; SOS is exempt (difficulty $0$). A relay verifies postage before
admitting a low-priority bundle, so a spammer pays $O(2^{k})$ hashes per message
while a genuine SOS pays nothing.

\subsection{Source-attributed custody reputation}\label{sec:custody}
Custody transfer~\cite{rfc5050} lets a committed custodian accept responsibility
for a bundle so an upstream node may free its copy. This creates a
\emph{black-hole} risk: a malicious custodian accepts custody and silently drops
the bundle. Detecting misbehavior in a DTN is hard because there is no path and
no timely acknowledgement.

Our contribution here, \abstr{Source-Attributed Custody Reputation}, exploits a
structural fact: delivery receipts are sealed to the \emph{original sender}, so
only the source can verify delivery. The source keeps a ledger mapping
$\text{bundle} \rightarrow \text{custodian}$ at each custody hand-off; when a
sealed delivery receipt arrives, it credits the custodians on that bundle's
path; a custodian that repeatedly takes custody of bundles that never deliver
(beyond a grace count) is demoted. Demotion only ever \emph{re-routes around} a
suspected node when an alternative exists---it never drops traffic---so the
$\geq 95\%$ delivery target is preserved even if the heuristic is wrong.

\built{Implemented in \texttt{router::attribution::ForwardLedger}. It is passive
(never alters store/forward) and source-local (no global reputation gossip to
poison), which sidesteps the Sybil/eclipse amplification that plagues shared
reputation systems (\S\ref{sec:security}).}

\section{Reliable Multi-Carrier Transfer and Swarm Fetch}\label{sec:swarm}
% ===========================================================================

The disaster requirement is that an object ride \emph{every} available carrier
at once and the recipient end up with \emph{all} of it, \emph{exactly once}, over
links that are lossy and intermittent. The internet's designers solved the
pieces of this long ago, and content addressing (\S\ref{sec:content}) already
supplies no-duplication and integrity while the manifest supplies a completeness
predicate. The remaining gap is \emph{multi-source} acquisition.

\abstr{Manifest-Scoped HAVE Swarm Fetch} closes it, adapting the BitTorrent
swarm~\cite{cohen2003bittorrent} to a DTN. For an object described by a manifest,
the fetcher:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=1pt]
  \item sends each candidate provider a \texttt{HaveQuery} listing its still-missing
  CIDs; a provider answers \texttt{HaveReply} with the subset it holds (a HAVE
  bitmap scoped to one object, not the whole store);
  \item requests each missing block from a provider known to hold it, with the
  choice offset by a per-fetch rotation counter and the block index, so distinct
  blocks are pulled from distinct providers in the same round;
  \item on retry, rotates each still-missing block to a \emph{different}
  provider, routing around a provider that has gone dark or is a black hole.
\end{enumerate}
Because at most one provider is asked per block per round, there is no duplicate
traffic; because blocks are content-addressed, any duplicate that does arrive is
idempotent; and because the missing set is monotone non-increasing, completion is
provable. This is the DTN analogue of a rarest-need, multi-source, carrier-
agnostic download.

\built{\texttt{NodeEngine::fetch\_content\_swarm}. Verified by two tests: two
providers each holding a disjoint half of the blocks (completion requires
combining both) and a primary provider that goes dark after first contact
(completion via the surviving provider). For \emph{whole-store} synchronization
between peers we instead use range-based set
reconciliation~\cite{minsky2003setrec,eppstein2011difference}
(crate \texttt{reconcile}), chosen over IBLTs for its deterministic,
no-silent-omission behavior.}

\section{Cryptographic Design}\label{sec:crypto}
% ===========================================================================

Lifeline composes audited primitives---Ed25519, X25519, HKDF-SHA256,
XChaCha20-Poly1305, BLAKE3~\cite{oconnor2020blake3}, and
Argon2id~\cite{biryukov2016argon2} for passphrase-wrapped key backup---and
invents no new primitive.

\paragraph{Sealed-sender forward-secret envelope.} A message is an
ephemeral-static sealed box (X25519 + HKDF + XChaCha20-Poly1305) to the
recipient, with the \emph{sender's signature placed inside the sealed
plaintext}, so relays learn neither content nor sender. Forward secrecy in the
present design comes not from a live ratchet---impractical when a sender may be
offline for days---but from a ring of \emph{rotating prekeys} that bounds the
window a seized long-term key can decrypt. This is a deliberate, documented
weakening relative to a full Double Ratchet~\cite{perrin2016doubleratchet}; the
X3DH-style asynchronous agreement~\cite{marlinspike2016x3dh} is the design
Lifeline approximates for the store-and-forward setting.

\paragraph{Groups and onion routing.} Groups use sender-keys (each member
distributes a symmetric sending key sealed to the others), so a message is
encrypted once and authenticated per sender. An optional onion
layer~\cite{dingledine2004tor} wraps a bundle so each hop learns only the next,
hiding the communication graph from any single relay.

\section{Discovery: Overlay, Geocast, and Time}\label{sec:discovery}
% ===========================================================================

\paragraph{Kademlia overlay.} When some nodes have connectivity, Lifeline builds
a Kademlia DHT~\cite{maymounkov2002kademlia} over the 128-bit address space
($k$-buckets, iterative $\alpha$-parallel \textsc{find\_node}/\textsc{find\_value})
for locating mailboxes and content providers. The DHT is transport-agnostic
(a \abstr{DhtRpc} request/response seam) and is explicitly \emph{not} relied upon
for offline operation.

\paragraph{Geocast.} Geohash~\cite{niemeyer2008geohash} maps a region to a
base-32 cell; a \emph{region-derived key} lets a sender address ``everyone in
cell $g$'' by deriving a deterministic keypair from the geohash. We are candid
that geocast confidentiality is weak by construction (the region commitment is
reversible), and document it as an addressing convenience, not a privacy
mechanism.

\paragraph{Differential time synchronization.} With no reachable NTP
server~\cite{rfc5905}, nodes agree on time by gossiping stratum-tagged beacons
and applying a bounded-slew median correction. TTL gating on relays requires the
caller to authenticate beacons first; this is enforced by API contract and noted
as a hardening dependency.

\section{Bridging Existing Ecosystems}\label{sec:bridge}
% ===========================================================================

Lifeline augments rather than replaces. The \abstr{ExternalNet} trait plus a
\abstr{BridgeInterface} adapter let any external network appear to the engine as
just another carrier. Two bridges are implemented: a live WebSocket
client to Nostr relays~\cite{nostrnip01} (using a domain-separated bearer subkey
so the Nostr identity is unlinkable to the mesh identity), and a Meshtastic
adapter speaking the ServiceEnvelope/MeshPacket protobuf over
MQTT~\cite{meshtastic}. Both features are off by default so the base build stays
lean.

\section{Capability-Governed Internet Egress}\label{sec:egress}
% ===========================================================================

A node with real internet can act as an \emph{exit} for others: the mesh becomes
the access network, that node the gateway, over a store-and-forward request/
response proxy (not a live socket, which a high-latency DTN cannot sustain). The
governing requirement is a clean split: \emph{mesh messages are open to all, but
internet egress is node-authorized}.

The naive model---an allow-list on the gateway---fails in a partition, because
the gateway cannot reach whatever server provisions the list. Every
comparable real-world system keeps the authority in a reachable server: cellular
PCRF, captive-portal RADIUS, and enterprise zero-trust
proxies~\cite{rose2020ztarch}. Our contribution, \abstr{Attenuable Egress
Capabilities}, instead puts the authority \emph{in a token the requester
presents}, following macaroons~\cite{birgisson2014macaroons} and
Biscuit~\cite{biscuit2021} and building on the Ed25519 we already ship. A
capability is a signed \emph{grant} plus a signature-chain of narrowing
\emph{attenuations}, with three properties:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=1pt]
  \item \textbf{Offline verification} --- a gateway that trusts the issuer's
  public key verifies the token with a signature check and a local clock; no
  server, works partitioned.
  \item \textbf{Narrow-only delegation} --- a holder appends a scope caveat
  (fewer hosts, tighter expiry, smaller byte cap) and passes the token on
  without contacting the issuer; the verifier conjoins every level, so a caveat
  can only \emph{remove} authority.
  \item \textbf{Unstrippable chain} --- each attenuation is signed by the key the
  previous level delegated to and binds the previous signature, so caveats cannot
  be reordered, spliced, or stripped.
\end{enumerate}
A \abstr{Service-Class} label ($\textsf{LifeSafety}\subset\textsf{Messaging}
\subset\textsf{Interactive}\subset\textsf{Bulk}$) tiers the egress, the
exit-side analogue of a 5G network slice or a DiffServ
class~\cite{rfc2475,foukas2017slicing}; the \textsf{Messaging} tier is the
zero-rated host-allowlist model familiar from in-flight Wi-Fi. The gateway
additionally enforces SSRF defenses~\cite{owasp_ssrf}: only
\texttt{http}/\texttt{https}, and never loopback, private~(RFC~1918),
link-local, carrier-grade-NAT~\cite{rfc6598}, or cloud-metadata targets, with the
real fetcher required to re-check the resolved IP against DNS rebinding. Real-time
calling (VoIP) is blocked \emph{structurally}: a request/response DTN has no
representation for a persistent bidirectional media stream.

\built{Crate \texttt{inet}. \abstr{AccessPolicy} is a policy-decision point;
\texttt{AllowList} is the trivial local issuer, \texttt{CapabilityPolicy} holds
only a set of trusted issuer keys. Twenty tests cover forgery, splicing,
stripping, expiry, subject-binding, monotone attenuation, offline verification
across a simulated partition, and the SSRF and byte-ceiling guards.}

\section{The Residual Differential Transfer Framework}\label{sec:diff}
% ===========================================================================

Several of Lifeline's optimizations are instances of one idea: never send what
the receiver can reconstruct from what it already has. Formally, to transfer a
quantity $Q$ when both parties share a reference $R$, transmit only the residual
\begin{equation}
  \Delta \;=\; Q - \mathbb{E}[Q \mid R],
\end{equation}
and let the receiver reconstruct $\hat{Q} = \mathbb{E}[Q\mid R] + \Delta$. Two
dual regimes follow. In the \emph{coding} regime $R$ is shared exactly and
$\Delta$ is a lossless delta (set reconciliation transmits $O(d\log|U|)$ for a
symmetric difference of size $d$~\cite{minsky2003setrec}; a manifest transmits
only missing CIDs). In the \emph{estimation} regime $R$ is a shared predictor and
$\Delta$ is a correction (differential time-sync transmits an offset against a
stratum reference, not an absolute clock; differential GPS transmits a local
correction against a common ephemeris). Recognizing these as one framework let us
reuse a single mental model---and a single validity check against the
information-theoretic literature---across time-sync, reconciliation, and content
delta, rather than re-deriving each. We flag that the framework is a design lens,
not a novel theorem; the underlying results are classical.

\section{Evaluation Methodology and Benchmarks}\label{sec:eval}
% ===========================================================================

\paragraph{What we measure today.} Lifeline ships an in-repo acceptance
simulation held to a $\geq 95\%$ delivery target across partition, churn, and
loss scenarios, plus $\sim$200 unit and integration tests exercising each
subsystem (crypto round-trips, custody, swarm fetch, capability verification).
This establishes functional correctness and a delivery floor, but it is
\emph{not} a comparative performance evaluation.

\paragraph{What a comparative evaluation requires.} The DTN literature evaluates
routing on a standard metric set: delivery ratio, delivery latency, overhead
(relay) ratio, buffer occupancy, and hop count~\cite{spyropoulos2005sprayandwait,
vahdat2000epidemic}. The de-facto tool is the ONE
simulator~\cite{keranen2009one}, driven by synthetic mobility (Random
Waypoint~\cite{broch1998comparison}, the Working Day Movement
Model~\cite{ekman2008workingday}) and real contact traces (the CRAWDAD
\texttt{cambridge/haggle} Bluetooth traces~\cite{crawdad_haggle}, MIT Reality
Mining~\cite{eagle2006realitymining}). Table~\ref{tab:metrics} lists the metrics
we plan to report and the comparison baselines (epidemic and spray-and-wait
routing, and where relevant Meshtastic and Briar's transport). This comparative
study on standard traces is the principal piece of empirical work still
outstanding.

\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}
% ===========================================================================

Lifeline sits at the confluence of DTN routing~\cite{rfc4838,vahdat2000epidemic,
spyropoulos2005sprayandwait}, content-addressed storage~\cite{benet2014ipfs,
cohen2003bittorrent}, secure messaging~\cite{marlinspike2016x3dh,
perrin2016doubleratchet}, peer-to-peer overlays~\cite{maymounkov2002kademlia},
and capability-based authorization~\cite{birgisson2014macaroons,biscuit2021}. It
differs from deployed mesh messengers in refusing to trust relays (cf.\ the
Bridgefy break~\cite{albrecht2021bridgefy}), from Meshtastic~\cite{meshtastic} in
providing end-to-end sealed-sender confidentiality and a routing/custody layer
rather than a flood, and from server-anchored systems in keeping identity,
routing, and \emph{authorization} all functional under partition. Its
anonymity goals are weaker than mixnets~\cite{vandenhooff2015vuvuzela,
piotrowska2017loopix}, a trade we make deliberately for latency and offline
operation.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}\label{sec:future}
% ===========================================================================

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em,itemsep=1pt]
  \item \textbf{Independent review.} No external cryptographic/protocol audit has
  been done; the capability token format and the forward-secrecy claim need it.
  \item \textbf{Comparative evaluation.} A study on standard traces
  (\S\ref{sec:eval}) against epidemic/spray-and-wait baselines is outstanding.
  \item \textbf{Egress quota and revocation.} Capabilities enforce a per-response
  byte ceiling but not yet a cumulative token-bucket quota, and rely on short
  expiry rather than revocation lists.
  \item \textbf{Metadata protection.} Traffic-analysis resistance is limited;
  integrating cover-traffic or mixing~\cite{piotrowska2017loopix} is future work.
  \item \textbf{Overlay hardening.} Eclipse/bucket-poisoning defenses for the DHT
  and grey-hole detection for custody remain to be strengthened.
  \item \textbf{Live wiring.} Time-sync, reconciliation, and geocast exist and
  are tested as crates but are not yet all driven by the live node.
\end{itemize}

% ===========================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
% ===========================================================================

Project Lifeline shows that a messenger can keep its identity, its delivery
guarantee, its confidentiality, and even its \emph{authorization decisions}
working when every server is gone---by moving each of these into the endpoints
and into the data itself. Content addressing gives integrity and dedup for free;
a manifest turns ``complete'' into a checkable predicate; swarm fetch turns every
peer and every carrier into a source; and attenuable capabilities turn internet
egress into a portable, offline-verifiable right. The remaining work is
empirical and adversarial: measure it against the field's benchmarks, and subject
its cryptography to independent attack.
